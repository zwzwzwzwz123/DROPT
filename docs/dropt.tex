\title{Guided-DiffFNO: Policy Optimization via Critic-Guided Diffusion Fourier Neural Operators in HVAC Control
}

\author{Wei Zou,Yu Sun, Haibo Zhou,~\IEEEmembership{Fellow,~IEEE}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Building HVAC control presents a complex optimization challenge, requiring the simultaneous balancing of energy efficiency, thermal comfort, and multi-zone coordination. Conventional reinforcement learning (RL) approaches struggle with this highly coupled environment, often producing unstable, high-frequency control actions due to their reliance on unimodal distributions and point-wise function approximators. To overcome these fundamental limitations, we propose Guided-DiffFNO, a novel generative control framework that synergizes conditional diffusion policies with spectral inductive biases. Instead of treating thermal zones independently, our method utilizes a Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) backbone to natively capture cross-zone spatial dependencies in the action space, naturally filtering out undesirable high-frequency control jitter. Furthermore, we integrate an inference-time critic-guided sampling mechanism, injecting value gradients directly into the reverse diffusion process to actively steer multi-zone actions toward safer, higher-reward regions. Evaluated in the physics-based BEAR simulation environment, Guided-DiffFNO demonstrates a superior energy-comfort trade-off compared to state-of-the-art RL baselines. Crucially, the integration of generative action modeling, spectral structure, and value-aware refinement yields significantly smoother and more physically consistent control trajectories, establishing a robust new paradigm for safe and efficient building control.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Building HVAC control, diffusion policy, Fourier neural operator, critic-guided sampling, reinforcement learning, thermal comfort, energy-efficient control.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Buildings account for a substantial share of global energy consumption, and heating, ventilation, and air-conditioning (HVAC) systems are among the dominant contributors to building operational energy use \cite{building_energy_reference,hvac_energy_reference}. Improving HVAC control is therefore of both practical and societal importance, as it directly affects energy efficiency, occupant comfort, and equipment operating stability. In modern commercial buildings, this problem is particularly challenging because thermal dynamics are strongly influenced by weather conditions, internal loads, occupancy patterns, and cross-zone heat transfer. As a result, high-performance HVAC control requires not only accurate local regulation, but also coordinated decision-making across multiple coupled thermal zones.

Classical control strategies, such as rule-based control, PID regulation, and model predictive control (MPC), have been widely studied for HVAC management. Rule-based and PID methods are easy to deploy, but their performance is often limited under highly dynamic operating conditions. MPC can explicitly account for future disturbances and physical constraints, and is frequently regarded as a strong baseline for building control. However, its practical effectiveness depends on model fidelity, online optimization efficiency, and computational tractability, all of which become increasingly difficult to maintain in high-dimensional multi-zone settings \cite{mpc_building_reference}. These limitations have motivated growing interest in reinforcement learning (RL), which offers a data-driven alternative for long-horizon sequential decision-making without requiring exact analytical models of the environment.

Recent RL-based HVAC studies have demonstrated the potential of deep actor--critic methods to improve energy performance while preserving thermal comfort \cite{sac_hvac_reference,rl_building_reference}. Nevertheless, standard RL policies still face several limitations in multi-zone building control. First, many continuous-control methods rely on deterministic mappings or unimodal Gaussian action distributions, which may be insufficient for representing the complex and potentially multi-modal structure of optimal HVAC decisions. Second, commonly used policy backbones, such as multilayer perceptrons (MLPs), treat action coordinates largely independently and thus fail to explicitly encode the coupled structure of zone-wise control signals. Third, these methods often produce unstable or highly oscillatory actions, especially when learning from sparse or delayed performance feedback. In building systems, such oscillatory behavior is undesirable because it may lead to unnecessary energy expenditure, comfort degradation, and poor physical plausibility.

Diffusion models have recently emerged as a promising class of generative models for decision-making and control \cite{diffusion_policy_reference,diffuser_reference}. By formulating action generation as an iterative denoising process, diffusion policies provide a richer modeling framework than conventional parametric policies and can represent complex action distributions without imposing restrictive unimodal assumptions. This property is particularly attractive for HVAC control, where multiple feasible action patterns may exist under the same thermal state due to coupled dynamics and disturbance uncertainty. However, directly applying a generic diffusion policy to building control is still insufficient. On the one hand, standard denoisers do not explicitly account for the structured coupling among zone-level control variables. On the other hand, unguided diffusion sampling remains primarily distribution-driven and does not necessarily bias generation toward actions with high long-term value or improved comfort compliance.

To address these issues, this paper proposes \emph{Guided-DiffFNO}, a generative control framework tailored for multi-zone building energy management. While preserving the expressive advantage of conditional diffusion models, we introduce two critical structural enhancements. First, we replace the standard MLP denoiser with an FNO-based architecture that operates explicitly on the action-dimension axis. This effectively imposes a spectral inductive bias, promoting globally coordinated, low-frequency control patterns across thermal zones. Second, we incorporate a zero-training-overhead critic guidance mechanism, utilizing the gradient of a learned double-critic value function to progressively steer the reverse denoising trajectory toward higher-value and lower-violation action regions. 

To stabilize the learning of this generative policy, we further develop a unified training framework that combines diffusion-consistent behavior cloning (utilizing expert MPC demonstrations) with conservative value-driven policy improvement. 

The main technical contributions of this work are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Spectral Action Modeling via DiffFNO:} We formulate a novel denoising architecture by deploying a Fourier Neural Operator across the \emph{action-dimension axis}. By transforming multi-zone action coordinates into the frequency domain, this backbone inherently enforces a low-frequency spatial inductive bias, systematically suppressing physically inconsistent high-frequency control chatter across adjacent thermal zones.
    \item \textbf{Inference-Time Critic Guidance:} We design a zero-training-overhead guidance mechanism for continuous building control. By injecting gradients from a conservative double-Q value function directly into the reverse diffusion sampling steps, we dynamically steer the generated action manifold towards higher-return and safety-compliant regions without destroying the original diffusion prior.
    \item \textbf{Expert-Regularized Generative RL Paradigm:} We develop a hybrid actor--critic training scheme tailored for diffusion policies. This framework seamlessly unifies diffusion-consistent behavior cloning (from MPC experts) to stabilize early learning with value-driven policy gradient optimization for asymptotic performance improvement.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Validation of Physical Consistency:} Comprehensive evaluations on the physics-based BEAR benchmark demonstrate that Guided-DiffFNO not only improves the Pareto frontier of energy efficiency and thermal comfort, but also uniquely generates smoother, globally coordinated control trajectories compared to state-of-the-art unimodal RL and unguided diffusion baselines.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~2 reviews related work. Section~3 introduces the system model. Section~4 presents the proposed Guided-DiffFNO method. Section~5 describes the experimental setup. Section~6 discusses the experimental results, and Section~7 concludes the paper.


\section{Related Works}

\subsection{Building HVAC Control Methods}
Building HVAC control has been extensively studied, with Model Predictive Control (MPC) representing the dominant traditional approach. MPC formulates control as a constrained optimization problem over a receding horizon, enabling explicit handling of thermal comfort constraints and energy objectives simultaneously \cite{serale2018mpc}. However, MPC performance is fundamentally limited by the accuracy of the underlying physical model and the trade-off between prediction horizon length and computational cost: short horizons sacrifice long-term optimality, while longer horizons incur prohibitive computation overhead \cite{mpc_computation}.

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has emerged as a promising alternative, eliminating the need for explicit physical models by learning control policies through environmental interaction. Methods based on DDPG and SAC have demonstrated meaningful energy savings while maintaining thermal comfort in single-zone and multi-zone settings \cite{zhou2022ddpg, fu2022multizone}. However, existing DRL approaches typically generate control actions for each zone independently, failing to account for the inter-zone thermal coupling inherent in multi-zone buildings. As adjacent zones exchange heat through walls and airflow, independent action generation leads to poorly coordinated setpoints and suboptimal global control performance. These limitations motivate the need for a control framework that explicitly models the spatial coupling structure of multi-zone action spaces.

\subsection{Diffusion Models for Decision Making}
Diffusion models have recently demonstrated remarkable capabilities in sequential decision making and control. Diffusion Policy \cite{chi2023diffusion} introduced the conditional denoising diffusion framework for robot visuomotor control, showing advantages in modeling multimodal action distributions, handling high-dimensional action spaces, and training stability, achieving an average 46.9\% performance improvement across twelve tasks. Subsequent works have extended diffusion models to offline reinforcement learning: Diffuser \cite{janner2022diffuser} modeled full trajectories as diffusion processes, while Diffusion-QL \cite{wang2023diffusionql} combined diffusion-based policy representation with Q-value guidance for offline policy optimization.

Despite these advances, diffusion models have not been applied to building HVAC control. Furthermore, the denoising networks in existing diffusion policies predominantly adopt MLP architectures, which lack the inductive bias necessary to model spatial coupling relationships across multiple control zones. This architectural limitation prevents standard Diffusion Policy from capturing the coordinated structure of multi-zone action spaces, motivating our replacement of the MLP backbone with a spatially-aware neural operator.

\subsection{Fourier Neural Operators}
The Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) \cite{li2021fno} was originally proposed for learning solution operators of parametric partial differential equations (PDEs), achieving solution speeds three orders of magnitude faster than traditional numerical solvers. FNO operates by combining global integral operators parameterized in the Fourier domain with local nonlinear activation functions, enabling efficient modeling of long-range spatial dependencies that characterize PDE solutions. Recent spectral analysis has further revealed that FNO exhibits a pronounced low-frequency bias: compared to standard CNNs, FNO more readily learns low-frequency components while naturally suppressing high-frequency noise \cite{qin2024spectral}.

We observe a structural analogy between PDE spatial coupling and multi-zone HVAC action coordination: just as PDE solutions must satisfy spatial continuity constraints across neighboring nodes, multi-zone control setpoints must respect inter-zone thermal coupling to achieve globally coherent control. Multi-zone buildings exhibit well-documented thermal coupling between adjacent zones through conductive and convective heat transfer \cite{thermal_coupling}. By replacing the denoising network with FNO, we leverage its global frequency-domain operations to generate all zone actions jointly, endowing each denoising step with an inherent low-frequency spatial structure. Notably, FNO's low-frequency bias—identified as a limitation in PDE solving contexts \cite{qin2024spectral}—becomes a desirable inductive bias in our setting, as coordinated HVAC setpoints are expected to share smooth, globally consistent structure across zones.

\subsection{Guided Diffusion for Control}
Classifier Guidance \cite{dhariwal2021diffusion} demonstrated that diffusion sampling can be steered at inference time by incorporating gradients from an auxiliary classifier, improving generation quality without retraining the diffusion model. This inference-time guidance paradigm is particularly attractive for control applications, as it decouples the guidance signal from the training procedure. Classifier-Free Guidance \cite{ho2022classifierfree} achieved similar effects by modifying the training process itself, but at the cost of requiring retraining when the guidance objective changes.

Recent works have begun exploring RL-based signals for guiding diffusion policies. Policy-Guided Diffusion \cite{jackson2024policy} applied policy gradient signals to steer offline RL trajectory generation toward high-return regions. Diffusion Actor-Critic (DAC) \cite{fang2024dac} incorporated Q-value gradients as a soft guidance term during training. However, these approaches either require training additional guidance networks or introduce guidance during training, incurring non-trivial overhead. In contrast, our Critic Guidance directly leverages the Q-network already present in the RL training framework to guide the denoising process at inference time, achieving safety-aware action refinement with strictly zero additional training cost. To our knowledge, Guided-DiffFNO is the first method to apply inference-time critic-guided diffusion to building energy control.


\section{System Model}\label{sec:System Model}

\begin{figure}[!t]
    \captionsetup{width=\linewidth}
    \centerline{\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/system model.png}}
    \caption{System Schematic of the Multi-Zone Building HVAC Control Problem}
    \label{fig:system_model}
\end{figure}

We formulate multi-zone building HVAC control as a discounted Markov decision process (MDP)
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, r, \gamma),
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{S}$ and $\mathcal{A}$ denote the state and action spaces, $\mathcal{P}$ is the transition kernel induced by the building simulator, $r$ is the reward function, and $\gamma \in (0,1]$ is the discount factor. At each control step $t$, the controller observes the current building state $\mathbf{s}_t \in \mathcal{S}$ and outputs a continuous joint control action $\mathbf{a}_t \in \mathcal{A}$ for all thermal zones. The control objective is to reduce HVAC energy consumption while maintaining indoor thermal comfort under coupled building dynamics and external disturbances.

\subsection{Building Thermal Environment}
We consider an $N$-zone building modeled in the physics-based BEAR simulator. The thermal evolution of the building is jointly affected by HVAC actuation, outdoor weather conditions, solar irradiance, ground temperature, and internal heat gains. Let $\mathbf{s}_t$ denote the system state, $\mathbf{a}_t$ the control action, and $\boldsymbol{\xi}_t$ the exogenous disturbance realization at time step $t$. The building dynamics can be written in compact form as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{s}_{t+1} = f(\mathbf{s}_t, \mathbf{a}_t, \boldsymbol{\xi}_t),
    \label{eq:dynamics}
\end{equation}
where $f(\cdot)$ is the transition function implemented by the underlying simulator. In this work, the simulator serves as the environment for policy learning, and the controller does not assume access to an explicit analytical form of $f(\cdot)$.

A key characteristic of the problem is that the dynamics are \emph{cross-zone coupled}: the temperature evolution of each zone depends not only on its local actuation but also on neighboring-zone interactions and shared disturbances. Therefore, the control problem should be treated as \emph{joint action generation} over all zones rather than a collection of independent single-zone decisions.

\subsection{State Space}
The state vector contains both thermal states and disturbance-related variables. For an $N$-zone building, the state at time step $t$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{s}_t =
    \left[
    \mathbf{T}_t^{\mathrm{zone}},
    T_t^{\mathrm{out}},
    \mathbf{G}_t,
    T_t^{\mathrm{ground}},
    \mathbf{L}_t
    \right]
    \in \mathbb{R}^{3N+2},
    \label{eq:state}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{T}_t^{\mathrm{zone}} \in \mathbb{R}^N$ denotes the indoor temperatures of all zones, $T_t^{\mathrm{out}} \in \mathbb{R}$ is the outdoor air temperature, $\mathbf{G}_t \in \mathbb{R}^N$ represents zone-associated solar irradiance features, $T_t^{\mathrm{ground}} \in \mathbb{R}$ is the ground temperature, and $\mathbf{L}_t \in \mathbb{R}^N$ denotes the internal thermal loads of all zones, such as occupancy- and equipment-related heat gains. Hence, the total state dimension is $3N+2$.

This state representation preserves the coupled thermal structure of the multi-zone building and provides sufficient information for control-oriented decision making.

\subsection{Action Space}
The control action is defined as a continuous HVAC command for each zone:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{a}_t =
    [a_{t,1}, a_{t,2}, \dots, a_{t,N}]
    \in [-1,1]^N.
    \label{eq:action}
\end{equation}
Each action component corresponds to the normalized HVAC actuation intensity of one zone. Negative values indicate cooling and positive values indicate heating. The action is bounded to ensure physically meaningful actuation and stable interaction with the simulator.

Importantly, $\mathbf{a}_t$ is a \emph{joint} control vector over all zones. This formulation is consistent with the coupled dynamics in \eqref{eq:dynamics} and emphasizes that coordinated multi-zone control is required for good performance.

Given the normalized action, the instantaneous HVAC power is computed from the zone-wise actuation map and the maximum equipment power. Let $m_i \ge 0$ denote the actuation scaling coefficient of zone $i$, obtained from the simulator, and let $P_{\max}$ denote the maximum unit power in watts. Then the total HVAC power at time step $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    P_t^{\mathrm{hvac}}
    =
    \frac{P_{\max}}{1000}
    \sum_{i=1}^{N} m_i |a_{t,i}|,
    \qquad [\mathrm{kW}],
    \label{eq:power}
\end{equation}
and the corresponding energy consumption over one control interval $\Delta t$ seconds is
\begin{equation}
    E_t^{\mathrm{hvac}}
    =
    P_t^{\mathrm{hvac}} \frac{\Delta t}{3600},
    \qquad [\mathrm{kWh}].
    \label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Thermal Comfort and Reward Design}
The control objective must balance energy efficiency and occupant comfort. Let $T^{\mathrm{ref}}$ denote the target indoor temperature and $\delta$ the allowed comfort tolerance. For each zone $i$, the temperature deviation is
\begin{equation}
    d_{t,i} = \left| T_{t,i}^{\mathrm{zone}} - T^{\mathrm{ref}} \right|.
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the mean comfort deviation and the number of comfort violations at time step $t$ are defined as
\begin{equation}
    D_t^{\mathrm{mean}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} d_{t,i},
    \label{eq:comfort_mean}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    V_t = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}(d_{t,i} > \delta),
    \label{eq:violation}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{I}(\cdot)$ is the indicator function. In addition, the maximum temperature deviation is
\begin{equation}
    D_t^{\mathrm{max}} = \max_{1 \le i \le N} d_{t,i},
    \label{eq:comfort_max}
\end{equation}
which is used as an auxiliary evaluation metric.

The simulator provides a base reward $r_t^{\mathrm{env}}$ that reflects the trade-off between energy usage and temperature regulation. To further discourage unsafe thermal deviations, we add an explicit comfort-violation penalty and apply reward scaling for stable learning. The final reward is defined as
\begin{equation}
    r_t = \rho \left( r_t^{\mathrm{env}} - \lambda V_t \right),
    \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
where $\lambda > 0$ is the violation penalty coefficient and $\rho > 0$ is a reward scaling factor. This reward design encourages the controller to reduce HVAC energy use while keeping all zone temperatures within the desired comfort range.

\subsection{Control Objective}
The goal is to learn a stochastic control policy $\pi(\mathbf{a}_t \mid \mathbf{s}_t)$ that maximizes the expected cumulative discounted return over an episode of horizon $T$:
\begin{equation}
    J(\pi)
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{\pi}
    \left[
    \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t r_t
    \right].
    \label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the learned controller should optimize the long-term trade-off among HVAC energy consumption, thermal comfort, and comfort-violation risk under disturbance uncertainty and cross-zone thermal coupling.

The formulation above also clarifies the main challenge of the problem: because the transition dynamics are coupled across zones, a high-quality policy must generate coordinated joint actions that are globally consistent with the building thermal field, rather than treating each zone as an isolated control channel.


\section{Problem Formulation and Solution}
\label{sec:method}

Building on the MDP formulation in Section~\ref{sec:System Model}, 
we highlight three distinctive challenges that make multi-zone HVAC 
control particularly demanding for standard continuous-control methods.

First, the state-action relationship is highly nonlinear because zone temperatures are jointly affected by cross-zone thermal coupling, weather disturbances, solar irradiance, and internal heat gains. Second, the action vector has an inherent spatial structure: each dimension corresponds to one thermal zone, and the action should therefore be generated as a coordinated joint control vector rather than as a set of independent commands. Third, actions that appear locally feasible may still be suboptimal from the perspective of long-term value, especially when the building operates near thermal comfort boundaries.

These characteristics motivate a policy class that is distributionally expressive, structurally aware, and amenable to value-based refinement during inference. To address these challenges, we propose Guided-DiffFNO, a diffusion-policy-based framework that combines conditional denoising, spectral action modeling, and critic-guided reverse sampling.

\subsection{Conditional Diffusion Policy for Continuous HVAC Control}

Instead of directly regressing a deterministic action from the current state, we model policy generation as a conditional denoising diffusion process over the action space. Let $\mathbf{x}_0 \in \mathbb{R}^N$ denote the clean action to be generated, and let $\mathbf{x}_k$ denote its noisy version at diffusion step $k \in \{1,\dots,K\}$, where $K$ is the diffusion horizon. The forward diffusion process is defined as
\begin{equation}
q(\mathbf{x}_k \mid \mathbf{x}_0)
=
\mathcal{N}
\left(
\mathbf{x}_k;
\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_k}\mathbf{x}_0,
(1-\bar{\alpha}_k)\mathbf{I}
\right),
\label{eq:forward_diffusion}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_k = 1-\beta_k$ and $\bar{\alpha}_k = \prod_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_i$. Equivalently, $\mathbf{x}_k$ can be sampled in closed form as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_k
=
\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_k}\mathbf{x}_0
+
\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_k}\boldsymbol{\epsilon},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).
\label{eq:noisy_action}
\end{equation}

In our implementation, the diffusion actor is trained under the behavior-cloning setting, and the denoiser is parameterized to \emph{directly predict the clean action} rather than the injected noise. Specifically, the denoiser is written as
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_k,k,\mathbf{s}_t),
\label{eq:x0_prediction}
\end{equation}
where $f_\theta(\cdot)$ takes as input the noisy action, the diffusion step, and the current building state.

Given the reconstructed clean action $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0$, the reverse posterior is written as
\begin{equation}
q(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}\mid \mathbf{x}_k,\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0)
=
\mathcal{N}
\left(
\mathbf{x}_{k-1};
\boldsymbol{\mu}_k(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0,\mathbf{x}_k),
\sigma_k^2 \mathbf{I}
\right),
\label{eq:reverse_posterior}
\end{equation}
with posterior mean
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\mu}_k(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0,\mathbf{x}_k)
=
c_1(k)\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + c_2(k)\mathbf{x}_k,
\label{eq:posterior_mean}
\end{equation}
where the coefficients are explicitly given by
\begin{equation}
c_1(k)=\frac{\beta_k\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{k-1}}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_k},
\qquad
c_2(k)=\frac{(1-\bar{\alpha}_{k-1})\sqrt{\alpha_k}}{1-\bar{\alpha}_k},
\label{eq:posterior_coeff}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\sigma_k^2 = \frac{\beta_k(1-\bar{\alpha}_{k-1})}{1-\bar{\alpha}_k}.
\label{eq:posterior_var}
\end{equation}
Starting from Gaussian noise $\mathbf{x}_K \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$, the policy iteratively samples $\mathbf{x}_{k-1}$ from $\mathbf{x}_k$ until the final action $\mathbf{x}_0$ is obtained. The final output is clipped into the feasible control range $[-1,1]^N$.

This formulation is particularly suitable for HVAC control. Unlike unimodal Gaussian policies, diffusion policies do not impose a restrictive parametric form on the action distribution. More importantly, the iterative denoising trajectory provides a natural interface for injecting structural priors and value-based guidance into the action generation process.

\subsection{DiffFNO Backbone}

A key limitation of conventional denoisers based on multilayer perceptrons is that they treat the action vector as a generic coordinate array and therefore do not explicitly exploit the structured coupling among zone-wise control signals. In multi-zone HVAC control, however, the action is not merely a collection of independent commands. Rather, it forms a coordinated control field shaped by coupled thermal interactions across zones. To encode this structure, we replace the standard denoising backbone with a Fourier neural operator (FNO), yielding the proposed \emph{DiffFNO} architecture.

Given a noisy action $\mathbf{x}_k \in \mathbb{R}^N$, the current diffusion step $k$, and the state $\mathbf{s}_t$, DiffFNO first constructs a state embedding and a diffusion-step embedding,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_s=\phi_s(\mathbf{s}_t),
\qquad
\mathbf{h}_k=\phi_k(k),
\end{equation}
which are concatenated and projected into a latent conditioning vector
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{h}_c = \phi_c([\mathbf{h}_s,\mathbf{h}_k]) \in \mathbb{R}^{w},
\end{equation}
where $w$ denotes the latent channel width. The noisy action is then lifted into a one-dimensional latent feature map,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}^{(0)} = \phi_{\mathrm{in}}(\mathbf{x}_k) \in \mathbb{R}^{w \times N},
\end{equation}
and the conditioning vector is injected into the latent representation.

For each spectral layer $\ell$, the latent feature is updated as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell+1)}
=
\sigma
\left(
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{spec}}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)})
+
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)})
+
\mathbf{h}_c
\right),
\label{eq:spectral_layer}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{spec}}^{(\ell)}$ denotes a one-dimensional spectral convolution, $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{pw}}^{(\ell)}$ denotes a pointwise $1\times 1$ convolution, and $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the activation function.

To make the spectral operation explicit, let $\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}^{(\ell)}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)})$ denote the real-to-complex Fourier transform of the latent feature along the action-dimension axis. For the first $M$ retained modes, the spectral branch applies learnable complex linear mappings:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(\ell)}_{:,m}
=
\mathbf{R}^{(\ell)}_{m}\widehat{\mathbf{Y}}^{(\ell)}_{:,m},
\qquad
m=1,\dots,M,
\label{eq:spectral_modes}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{R}^{(\ell)}_{m} \in \mathbb{C}^{w\times w}$ is the complex weight matrix associated with mode $m$. Higher modes are discarded:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(\ell)}_{:,m}=\mathbf{0},
\qquad
m>M.
\label{eq:mode_truncation}
\end{equation}
The spectral output is then obtained through inverse Fourier transform:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{spec}}^{(\ell)}(\mathbf{Y}^{(\ell)})
=
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{Z}}^{(\ell)}\right).
\label{eq:spec_conv}
\end{equation}

It is important to emphasize that the FNO in this work operates along the \emph{action-dimension axis}, rather than along the temporal axis. Thus, it does not explicitly model temporal action trajectories. Instead, it imposes a spectral inductive bias directly on the multi-zone action vector, encouraging globally coordinated low-frequency control patterns and suppressing spatially inconsistent high-frequency oscillations across zones. This design is well aligned with the physical nature of multi-zone HVAC control, where mutually conflicting zone commands are generally undesirable.

After the spectral stack, the latent feature is projected back to the action space to produce the spectral denoising output
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_k = \phi_{\mathrm{out}}(\mathbf{Y}^{(L)}).
\end{equation}
To preserve local flexibility and avoid excessive over-smoothing, we further introduce a residual linear pathway
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{r}_k = \mathbf{W}_r \mathbf{x}_k,
\end{equation}
and define the final denoiser output as
\begin{equation}
f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_k,k,\mathbf{s}_t)
=
\hat{\mathbf{y}}_k + \mathrm{sigmoid}(g)\mathbf{r}_k,
\label{eq:final_denoiser}
\end{equation}
where $g$ is a learnable scalar gate. The residual pathway allows the model to retain local action variations useful for zone-specific regulation, while the spectral branch provides a global coordination prior. In this way, DiffFNO improves both expressiveness and structural consistency.

\subsection{Critic-Guided Reverse Sampling}

Although the diffusion policy can generate physically plausible actions, plausibility alone does not guarantee that the generated action is optimal with respect to long-term control performance. In particular, near thermal comfort boundaries, unguided reverse sampling may still converge to feasible but suboptimal actions. To alleviate this issue, we introduce critic-guided reverse sampling.

Let $Q_{\phi_1}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})$ and $Q_{\phi_2}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})$ denote the two critic networks, and define the conservative value estimate as
\begin{equation}
Q_{\phi}^{\min}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})
=
\min\left\{
Q_{\phi_1}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a}),
Q_{\phi_2}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})
\right\}.
\label{eq:qmin}
\end{equation}
At reverse diffusion step $k$, the actor first reconstructs the clean action estimate $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = f_\theta(\mathbf{x}_k,k,\mathbf{s}_t)$. The gradient of the conservative critic value with respect to the reconstructed action is then computed as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{g}_k
=
\nabla_{\mathbf{a}} Q_{\phi}^{\min}(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a})
\big|_{\mathbf{a}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0}.
\label{eq:qgrad}
\end{equation}
This gradient is injected into the reverse chain by correcting the reconstructed action,
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0
=
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \eta \mathbf{g}_k,
\label{eq:guided_x0}
\end{equation}
where $\eta > 0$ is the guidance scale. In the implementation, this is equivalently written as
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0
=
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 - \eta \mathbf{d}_k,
\qquad
\mathbf{d}_k = -\mathbf{g}_k,
\label{eq:guided_impl}
\end{equation}
which corresponds to subtracting the negative value gradient. The corrected clean action is clipped into the feasible range and substituted into the posterior mean,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\mu}_k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0,\mathbf{x}_k)
=
c_1(k)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0 + c_2(k)\mathbf{x}_k.
\label{eq:guided_mean}
\end{equation}
The reverse sampling process then proceeds accordingly.

This design has two advantages. First, critic guidance is integrated \emph{within} the reverse diffusion dynamics rather than applied as a post hoc correction after action generation. Consequently, value information influences the denoising trajectory throughout the sampling process. Second, because thermal comfort violations are already reflected in the reward, the learned critic implicitly discourages actions likely to induce unsafe temperature deviations. Therefore, critic guidance improves value alignment and empirically promotes comfort-compliant action generation. However, it should be interpreted as value-guided refinement rather than as a formal hard-safety guarantee.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Critic-guided reverse sampling for Guided-DiffFNO}
\label{alg:guided_sampling}
\KwIn{Current state $\mathbf{s}$, denoiser $f_{\theta}$, critics $Q_{\phi_1}$ and $Q_{\phi_2}$, diffusion horizon $K$, guidance scale $\eta$.}
\KwOut{Generated action $\mathbf{x}_0^\ast$.}

Sample initial noise $\mathbf{x}_K \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$\;

\For{$k = K, K-1, \ldots, 1$}{
    Predict clean action estimate $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 = f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_k,k,\mathbf{s})$\;
    Compute conservative critic value
    $Q_{\phi}^{\min}(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0)=\min\{Q_{\phi_1}(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0),Q_{\phi_2}(\mathbf{s},\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0)\}$\;
    Compute value gradient
    $\mathbf{g}_k = \nabla_{\mathbf{a}} Q_{\phi}^{\min}(\mathbf{s},\mathbf{a})|_{\mathbf{a}=\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0}$\;
    Correct the reconstructed action
    $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0 = \mathrm{clip}(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_0 + \eta \mathbf{g}_k,-1,1)$\;
    Compute posterior mean
    $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k = c_1(k)\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0 + c_2(k)\mathbf{x}_k$\;
    \eIf{$k > 1$}{
        Sample Gaussian noise $\mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$\;
        Update reverse sample
        $\mathbf{x}_{k-1} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_k + \sigma_k \mathbf{z}$\;
    }{
        Set $\mathbf{x}_0^\ast = \mathrm{clip}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,-1,1)$\;
    }
}
\Return{$\mathbf{x}_0^\ast$}\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Critic Learning}

The value function is learned using a double-critic architecture. For each state-action pair $(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t)$, the critic outputs two Q-values,
\begin{equation}
Q_{\phi_1}(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t),
\qquad
Q_{\phi_2}(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t).
\end{equation}
To reduce overestimation bias, target values are computed using the minimum of the two target critics. Specifically, for the bootstrap state $\mathbf{s}_{t+n}$, the target actor generates the next action $\mathbf{a}'_{t+n}$, and the target Q-value is defined as
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
Q_{\phi'}^{\min}(\mathbf{s}_{t+n},\mathbf{a}'_{t+n})
&= \min\Big\{ Q_{\phi_1'}(\mathbf{s}_{t+n},\mathbf{a}'_{t+n}), \\
&\quad\ Q_{\phi_2'}(\mathbf{s}_{t+n},\mathbf{a}'_{t+n}) \Big\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Using $n$-step return estimation, the target is written as
\begin{equation}
y_t^{(n)} = \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\gamma^i r_{t+i} + \gamma^n Q_{\phi'}^{\min}(\mathbf{s}_{t+n},\mathbf{a}'_{t+n}).
\label{eq:nstep_target}
\end{equation}
The critic objective is then
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{critic}} 
&= \mathbb{E}\left[\left(Q_{\phi_1}(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t)-y_t^{(n)}\right)^2\right] \\
&\quad + \mathbb{E}\left[\left(Q_{\phi_2}(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t)-y_t^{(n)}\right)^2\right].
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:critic_loss}
\end{equation}

The double-critic design is particularly important here because the critic is used not only for policy improvement during training but also as the guidance source during reverse diffusion inference. A more conservative value surface therefore benefits both training stability and inference quality.

\subsection{Actor Learning with Expert-Regularized Policy Optimization}

The actor is optimized through a combination of diffusion-based expert regularization and critic-driven policy improvement. Let $\pi_{\mathrm{exp}}$ denote the expert controller. In the final implementation, the expert label is aligned with the \emph{current} state, namely
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{a}_t^{\mathrm{exp}}
=
\pi_{\mathrm{exp}}(\mathbf{s}_t).
\label{eq:expert_alignment}
\end{equation}
This alignment avoids temporal mismatch between the supervised label and the policy input state.

Under the diffusion actor's clean-action prediction parameterization, a diffusion step $k$ is first sampled uniformly, and Gaussian noise is injected into the expert action:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{exp}}
=
\sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{k}}\,\mathbf{a}_t^{\mathrm{exp}}
+
\sqrt{1-\bar{\alpha}_{k}}\,\boldsymbol{\epsilon},
\qquad
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).
\label{eq:expert_noisy}
\end{equation}
The denoiser is then trained to reconstruct the clean expert action:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BC}}
=
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t^{\mathrm{exp}},k,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
\left[
\left\|
f_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\mathrm{exp}},k,\mathbf{s}_t)
-
\mathbf{a}_t^{\mathrm{exp}}
\right\|_2^2
\right].
\label{eq:bc_loss}
\end{equation}
This objective is exactly consistent with the diffusion actor used in our implementation: the noisy expert action is the input, and the clean expert action is the training target.

In addition to expert regularization, the actor is optimized to maximize the conservative critic value. For a sampled state $\mathbf{s}_t$, the actor generates an action through reverse diffusion, denoted as $\pi_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t)$, and the policy-improvement term is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PG}}
=
-
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{s}_t}
\left[
Q_{\phi}^{\min}(\mathbf{s}_t,\pi_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t))
\right].
\label{eq:pg_loss}
\end{equation}
The final actor objective is a weighted combination of the two terms:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{actor}}
=
\omega \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BC}}
+
(1-\omega)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PG}},
\label{eq:actor_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\omega \in [0,1]$ is the behavior-cloning weight. In practice, $\omega$ is initialized with a relatively large value and then linearly decayed toward a smaller terminal value during training. Let $u$ denote the actor update step and $H$ denote the decay horizon. The schedule is
\begin{equation}
\omega(u)
=
\omega_0
+
\min\left(\frac{u}{H},1\right)(\omega_f-\omega_0),
\label{eq:bc_schedule}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_0$ and $\omega_f$ are the initial and final behavior-cloning weights, respectively. This schedule stabilizes early learning through expert supervision while gradually allowing the policy to move beyond imitation and optimize long-term control performance.

After each update, both the target actor and target critics are updated using Polyak averaging:
\begin{equation}
\theta' \leftarrow \tau_{\mathrm{p}} \theta + (1-\tau_{\mathrm{p}})\theta',
\qquad
\phi' \leftarrow \tau_{\mathrm{p}} \phi + (1-\tau_{\mathrm{p}})\phi',
\label{eq:polyak}
\end{equation}
where $\tau_{\mathrm{p}}$ is the soft update rate.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Training procedure of Guided-DiffFNO}
\label{alg:training_guided_difffno}
\KwIn{Replay buffer $\mathcal{B}$, actor $\pi_\theta$, target actor $\pi_{\theta'}$, critics $Q_{\phi_1}$ and $Q_{\phi_2}$, target critics $Q_{\phi_1'}$ and $Q_{\phi_2'}$, initial BC weight $\omega_0$, final BC weight $\omega_f$, decay horizon $H$.}
\KwOut{Trained actor $\pi_\theta$, critics $Q_{\phi_1}$ and $Q_{\phi_2}$.}

Initialize actor and critics, and copy them to the target networks\;
Initialize the behavior-cloning weight $\omega \leftarrow \omega_0$\;

\While{the stopping criterion is not met}{
    Collect transitions $(\mathbf{s}_t,\mathbf{a}_t,r_t,\mathbf{s}_{t+1},\mathrm{info}_t)$ and store them in $\mathcal{B}$\;
    Sample a minibatch from $\mathcal{B}$\;

    Generate target action $\mathbf{a}'_{t+n} = \pi_{\theta'}(\mathbf{s}_{t+n})$\;
    Compute conservative target value
    $Q_{\phi'}^{\min}(\mathbf{s}_{t+n},\mathbf{a}'_{t+n})
    =
    \min\{Q_{\phi_1'}(\mathbf{s}_{t+n},\mathbf{a}'_{t+n}),Q_{\phi_2'}(\mathbf{s}_{t+n},\mathbf{a}'_{t+n})\}$\;
    Compute the $n$-step target using \eqref{eq:nstep_target}\;
    Update the critics by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{critic}}$\;

    Generate actor action $\tilde{\mathbf{a}}_t = \pi_\theta(\mathbf{s}_t)$\;
    Compute policy-improvement loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PG}}$ using \eqref{eq:pg_loss}\;

    \eIf{expert action $\mathbf{a}_t^{\mathrm{exp}}$ exists in $\mathrm{info}_t$}{
        Sample diffusion step $k \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\dots,K\}$\;
        Sample Gaussian noise $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$\;
        Construct noisy expert action using \eqref{eq:expert_noisy}\;
        Compute expert-regularization loss $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BC}}$ using \eqref{eq:bc_loss}\;
        Set mixed actor loss
        $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{actor}} = \omega \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{BC}} + (1-\omega)\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PG}}$\;
    }{
        Set $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{actor}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{PG}}$\;
    }

    Update actor parameters by minimizing $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{actor}}$\;
    Soft-update target networks using \eqref{eq:polyak}\;
    Update $\omega$ according to \eqref{eq:bc_schedule}\;
}
\Return{Trained actor and critics}\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Inference Procedure}

During deployment, the current building state $\mathbf{s}_t$ is fed to the diffusion actor, which initializes the reverse chain from Gaussian noise $\mathbf{x}_K \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$. For each reverse step $k=K,K-1,\ldots,1$, the DiffFNO backbone predicts the clean action estimate, critic guidance is injected when enabled, and the posterior $q(\mathbf{x}_{k-1}\mid \mathbf{x}_k,\tilde{\mathbf{x}}_0)$ is sampled. After the final reverse step, the resulting action $\mathbf{x}_0$ is clipped to $[-1,1]^N$ and applied to the building environment.

The resulting controller combines three complementary mechanisms: diffusion-based generative action modeling, spectral structure learning in the multi-zone action space, and critic-guided value refinement during sampling. Together, these components enable the policy to generate actions that are smoother, more coordinated, and better aligned with long-term HVAC performance objectives.

\subsection{Overall Energy--Comfort Trade-off}

Table~\ref{tab:overall_results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:energy_violations} summarize the main quantitative results. The Pareto view is particularly informative here because the HVAC task is intrinsically bi-objective: a controller is only practically meaningful if it reduces energy use while simultaneously maintaining comfort compliance. Under this view, the non-dominated frontier in Fig.~\ref{fig:energy_violations} is formed by three diffusion-based variants only, namely DiffFNO w/o Residual \& Guidance, DiffFNO w/o Residual, and Guided-DiffFNO. All other displayed methods are dominated in the energy--comfort plane. In particular, DiffFNO w/o Guidance is shown in the scatter plot as a dominated reference point, but it is not connected by the dashed Pareto line because DiffFNO w/o Residual \& Guidance improves both objectives simultaneously, reducing energy from $895.7$~kWh to $861.5$~kWh and violations from $1.380$ to $1.002$.

Among these frontier methods, Guided-DiffFNO occupies the most desirable safety-oriented end of the trade-off curve. It achieves the lowest comfort violation score of $0.605$ and the highest final test reward of $-0.557$, while keeping the average HVAC energy at $877.3$~kWh. By contrast, DiffFNO w/o Residual \& Guidance attains the lowest raw energy value ($861.5$~kWh), but does so at substantially worse comfort compliance ($1.002$ violations) and lower final reward ($-0.794$). This distinction is important: from the perspective of building control, minimizing HVAC power alone is not a sufficient objective, because a useful controller must optimize energy and thermal safety jointly rather than overfitting to one side of the trade-off. Guided-DiffFNO therefore provides the best balanced solution among all compared methods.

The contribution of critic-guided reverse sampling is directly visible in this comparison. Relative to the unguided DiffFNO variant, Guided-DiffFNO reduces energy consumption from $895.7$~kWh to $877.3$~kWh and cuts comfort violations from $1.380$ to $0.605$, corresponding to a $2.1\%$ reduction in energy and a $56.2\%$ reduction in violations. The final test reward also improves from $-1.037$ to $-0.557$. Notably, the unguided model does not lie on the Pareto frontier because it is simultaneously outperformed by DiffFNO w/o Residual \& Guidance in both energy and comfort. This observation indicates that value-guided refinement is not merely a cosmetic adjustment to the final sampled action, but materially changes the denoising trajectory toward higher-value and safer control decisions.

The comparison with non-spectral and conventional baselines is even more pronounced. Relative to the MLP-based diffusion policy, Guided-DiffFNO reduces energy by $20.7\%$ and comfort violations by $58.3\%$. Relative to MPC, the proposed method lowers energy by $12.7\%$ and violations by $66.0\%$. The excluded SAC and SAC+MPC baselines, although omitted from Fig.~\ref{fig:energy_violations} for scale clarity, remain important as negative references in Table~\ref{tab:overall_results}: SAC consumes $5897.0$~kWh with $5.680$ violations, while SAC+MPC still consumes $1934.6$~kWh with $3.462$ violations. These results indicate that standard unimodal actor policies, even when aided by expert regularization, remain poorly matched to the strongly coupled multi-zone HVAC control problem.

\subsection{Training Dynamics and Optimization Stability}

The training curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:reward_curves} reveal a clear difference in optimization stability. Guided-DiffFNO is the only method that maintains a strong reward level throughout most of training and finishes with the best final performance. Its reward already improves to approximately $-0.578$ at $2.5\times 10^5$ environment steps, remains around the $-0.53$ to $-0.60$ range over the second half of training, and reaches its best checkpoint value of $-0.453$ near $7.99\times 10^5$ steps.

By contrast, the unguided DiffFNO variant exhibits much larger oscillations and finishes significantly worse than the guided model. The MLP-based diffusion policy also fails to maintain stable improvement and gradually degrades as training proceeds. SAC+MPC shows a strong initial reward due to expert regularization, but this advantage quickly collapses; after an early best value of $-0.391$ around $7.37\times 10^4$ steps, its performance steadily deteriorates and ends at a much lower level than Guided-DiffFNO. This behavior suggests that imitation alone is insufficient to stabilize long-horizon policy improvement unless the policy class itself can preserve a structured action prior. Pure SAC is unstable from the beginning and never enters the reward regime reached by the diffusion-based methods.

These results indicate that the combination of diffusion-based action modeling, FNO-induced structural bias, and inference-time critic refinement leads to a better optimization landscape than either conventional actor-critic training or unguided denoising alone.

Figure~\ref{fig:q_mc_return} addresses this point by plotting the critic-estimated $Q(s,a)$ against the corresponding Monte Carlo return on logged evaluation samples. This result should not be interpreted as perfect critic calibration; such a claim would be too strong for an off-policy continuous-control setting. However, it does show that the learned critic preserves a useful ranking relationship between actions with lower and higher long-term return. From the perspective of guided diffusion, this is the relevant property: the critic does not need to provide an exact return estimator at every point in action space, but it does need to induce gradients that preferentially move the denoising process toward higher-value regions. The significant improvement of Guided-DiffFNO over unguided DiffFNO in both reward and comfort violations, together with the positive $Q$--return relation in Fig.~\ref{fig:q_mc_return}, therefore supports the claim that critic guidance is functioning as an effective inference-time refinement mechanism.

\subsection{Action Quality and Physical Consistency}

Beyond scalar reward and energy metrics, the proposed method also produces substantially smoother and more physically plausible control trajectories. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:action_quality}, Guided-DiffFNO achieves the lowest action-variation level among the evaluated learned controllers, with mean absolute action change $0.0244$. This is nearly identical to the no-residual guided variant, but much lower than unguided DiffFNO ($0.0471$), Diffusion-MLP ($0.0964$), SAC+MPC ($0.1656$), and SAC ($0.3970$). Relative to Diffusion-MLP, the full model reduces average action variation by $74.7\%$, which confirms that the FNO backbone is effective in suppressing control chatter in the joint action space.

Figure~\ref{fig:multizone_coordination} provides a direct visualization of the cross-zone coordination claim that motivates the FNO backbone. This figure is particularly important because the single-room trajectories in Fig.~\ref{fig:traj_control} alone could otherwise be interpreted as a representative but potentially cherry-picked local example. Over the same 72-hour window, the six zone actions generated by Guided-DiffFNO evolve in a highly synchronized manner, with mean pairwise inter-zone correlation $0.960$ and mean cross-zone standard deviation $0.024$. The high correlation should not be interpreted as the policy collapsing all zone commands to an identical scalar profile. Rather, it indicates that different zones respond coherently to the same global disturbances and operating rhythm, such as weather and daily load cycles, while still retaining nonzero zone-specific variation. This remaining differentiation is reflected by the nonzero cross-zone standard deviation, which stays at $0.024$ for Guided-DiffFNO and is substantially smaller than the much less structured $0.122$ observed for the MLP-based diffusion policy.

Importantly, adding DiffFNO w/o Guidance to the same visualization clarifies that the structural benefit is not created solely by critic guidance. The unguided DiffFNO baseline already exhibits relatively strong inter-zone coordination, with mean pairwise correlation $0.891$ and mean cross-zone standard deviation $0.030$, placing it clearly between Guided-DiffFNO and Diffusion-MLP. This three-way comparison therefore supports a more precise interpretation of the method components: the FNO backbone is the main source of coordinated multi-zone action structure, whereas critic guidance further sharpens that structure and improves the final energy--comfort trade-off.

In contrast, the MLP-based diffusion policy exhibits much weaker agreement across zones, with mean pairwise correlation only $0.212$. This comparison directly supports the structural hypothesis of the paper: the FNO backbone is not merely smoother in a generic sense, but specifically better at producing joint control vectors that remain globally coherent across coupled thermal zones without degenerating to identical outputs.

The frequency-domain analysis further supports this conclusion. Let $\{f_m\}_{m=1}^{M}$ denote the discrete Welch frequency bins and let $S(f_m)$ denote the averaged spectrum shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:action_quality}. Because the stored PSD is discrete rather than continuous, the reported high-frequency ratio is computed by the same discrete summation used in the figure post-processing:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{\mathrm{HF}}=\frac{\sum_{m:\,f_m>2\ \mathrm{cpd}} S(f_m)}{\sum_{m=1}^{M} S(f_m)},
\label{eq:hf_ratio}
\end{equation}
where ``cpd'' denotes cycles per day. Under the hourly control interval, the cutoff of $2$ cycles/day corresponds to oscillations faster than one cycle every 12 hours. We use this threshold as an explicit analysis convention in this paper to distinguish daily or slower actuation structure from visibly faster control chatter; it should therefore be interpreted as an operational cutoff for comparing policies, rather than as a universal physical constant. With this discrete definition, Guided-DiffFNO allocates $11.4\%$ of its spectral mass to the high-frequency region, compared with $25.8\%$ for unguided DiffFNO and $57.8\%$ for Diffusion-MLP. All learned methods still exhibit a dominant spectral peak around $1.125$ cycles/day, which is consistent with the daily rhythm of building thermal disturbances. Therefore, the proposed approach does not remove the meaningful low-frequency control structure associated with building operation; instead, it selectively suppresses spurious high-frequency oscillations that are unlikely to be physically desirable.

The time-domain trajectories in Fig.~\ref{fig:traj_control} provide a concrete room-level illustration of the same behavior. Over the representative evaluation window, the selected room under Guided-DiffFNO leaves the comfort band only $3$ times out of $72$ control steps, whereas the unguided DiffFNO leaves the band $17$ times and the Diffusion-MLP controller produces far larger excursions. In the same window, the room-wise action sequence generated by Guided-DiffFNO has mean step-to-step variation $0.0259$, compared with $0.0415$ for unguided DiffFNO, $0.1384$ for Diffusion-MLP, and $0.4387$ for SAC. SAC also exhibits extreme temperature excursions, with the selected-room state ranging from $-9.10^\circ$C to $43.15^\circ$C. These values should be interpreted as raw simulator state outputs under destabilizing control inputs after the closed-loop system has left the normal operating regime, rather than as realistic occupied-zone temperatures in a physically meaningful HVAC trajectory. In other words, they are evidence of severe control-induced instability in the simulator response, which is precisely why we use them as a symptom of poor physical consistency.

Taken together, these results support the central design claim of this paper: for multi-zone HVAC control, a good policy should not only optimize aggregate reward, but should also generate coordinated joint actions whose temporal and spectral characteristics remain compatible with the physics of coupled building dynamics.

\subsection{Ablation Analysis}

Figure~\ref{fig:ablation_heatmap} and Table~\ref{tab:overall_results} clarify the role of each component in Guided-DiffFNO.

To avoid ambiguity, the normalized scores in Fig.~\ref{fig:ablation_heatmap} are not absolute performance values. They are computed by column-wise min--max normalization over the five ablation variants only. Specifically, energy uses the average episode HVAC energy, comfort uses the comfort satisfaction rate $1-\text{violations}/(T\times N)$, smoothness uses the mean squared action increment, and convergence uses the area under the test-reward curve. For metrics where smaller raw values are better, namely energy and smoothness, we first reverse the direction and then apply min--max scaling. Consequently, a value of $0.00$ means that a method is the lowest-performing one on that metric within the ablation set, while $1.00$ means that it is the strongest one. The fact that Diffusion-MLP appears as $0.00$ on all four axes therefore indicates that it is the weakest ablation baseline under this normalization, not that it was artificially fixed as a reference lower bound.

First, replacing the conventional MLP denoiser with the FNO backbone already yields a substantial gain in action quality. Comparing Diffusion-MLP with DiffFNO w/o Guidance, energy decreases from $1106.2$~kWh to $895.7$~kWh, violations decrease from $1.449$ to $1.380$, and mean action variation drops from $0.0964$ to $0.0471$. The largest gain here is in smoothness, which is exactly what should be expected from a spectral backbone that directly models cross-zone action structure rather than treating each action dimension independently.

Second, critic-guided reverse sampling is the dominant factor behind the comfort improvement. Comparing Guided-DiffFNO with DiffFNO w/o Guidance, the action smoothness improves by $48.2\%$ and violations drop by $56.2\%$, while energy also improves slightly. This shows that guidance is not simply a cosmetic refinement at the final action stage; it changes the denoising trajectory in a way that consistently biases sampling toward safer and higher-value regions.

Third, the residual pathway mainly improves local controllability and the final energy--comfort balance. Removing the residual branch while keeping guidance leads to slightly lower energy ($866.9$~kWh) but worse comfort ($0.707$ violations) and lower reward ($-0.615$). In other words, the residual connection does not serve as the main source of smoothness, because the spectral branch already provides that property; instead, it restores enough local flexibility to correct room-specific errors near the comfort boundary.

Overall, the ablation results are internally consistent with the proposed method design: the FNO backbone contributes structural smoothness, critic guidance contributes value alignment and safety-aware refinement, and the residual branch recovers local adaptability. Their combination yields the most balanced controller.

\subsection{Discussion}

From a control perspective, the experimental evidence suggests that the advantage of Guided-DiffFNO does not come from a single metric-specific trick. Instead, it comes from matching the policy class to the underlying problem structure. Multi-zone HVAC control is a coupled, safety-sensitive, and distributionally multi-modal decision problem. Under this view, point-estimate policies such as SAC are mismatched because they cannot represent diverse but coordinated action patterns; plain diffusion with an MLP backbone is insufficient because it lacks an explicit inductive bias for cross-zone structure; and unguided diffusion is incomplete because distributional plausibility does not guarantee value-optimality. Guided-DiffFNO resolves these three mismatches jointly, which explains why it achieves the most favorable compromise among energy efficiency, comfort preservation, convergence behavior, and action smoothness.







\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper proposed Guided-DiffFNO, a generative reinforcement learning framework for multi-zone building HVAC control. The method was developed from a first-principles view of the control problem: high-quality HVAC control should not be treated as independent point-wise action regression, but as structured joint decision making over a coupled thermal field. Based on this perspective, we combined three complementary ingredients in a unified policy optimization framework. First, a conditional diffusion policy was adopted to model complex continuous action distributions beyond conventional unimodal actor parameterizations. Second, an FNO-based denoising backbone was introduced to encode cross-zone spatial coupling directly in the action space and to impose a useful low-frequency inductive bias for coordinated control generation. Third, an inference-time critic-guidance mechanism was incorporated into the reverse diffusion process so that value information could refine sampled actions toward safer and higher-return regions without introducing additional training overhead.

Experiments on the BEAR physics-based building benchmark demonstrated that the proposed approach achieves a more favorable energy--comfort trade-off than both conventional RL baselines and alternative diffusion-policy variants. In particular, Guided-DiffFNO obtained the best overall test reward and the lowest comfort violation level, while maintaining low HVAC energy consumption. Compared with SAC, MPC, and MLP-based diffusion control, the proposed method generated smoother and more physically consistent control trajectories, substantially reduced high-frequency action oscillations, and exhibited better optimization stability during training. The ablation studies further clarified that the FNO backbone mainly contributes structural smoothness and cross-zone coordination, critic-guided reverse sampling is the key factor behind improved comfort compliance and value alignment, and the residual pathway helps preserve local flexibility near comfort boundaries. These results consistently support the central claim of this work: generative action modeling, spectral structure learning, and value-guided refinement are jointly necessary for robust multi-zone HVAC control.

More broadly, this work suggests that diffusion-based control is not only a richer policy class, but also a practical mechanism for injecting physical structure and decision-time guidance into building energy management. Rather than relying on a purely reactive point-estimate controller, the proposed framework performs iterative action refinement in a way that better matches the coupled, safety-sensitive, and multi-modal nature of building thermal control.

Several directions remain for future work. First, the current study focuses on a fixed benchmark setting, and broader evaluation across different building types, climates, and disturbance profiles is needed to assess generalization. Second, although critic guidance empirically improves safety-related behavior, it should still be interpreted as value-guided refinement rather than a formal hard-constraint guarantee; integrating explicit constraint handling or safety shielding would be an important next step. Third, extending the present framework from single-step joint action generation to longer-horizon trajectory-level generative planning may further improve anticipation of delayed thermal dynamics. Finally, transferring the proposed method from simulation to real building deployment will require addressing model mismatch, sensing uncertainty, and online adaptation. We believe these directions will further strengthen the role of structured generative control in safe and energy-efficient building operation.
